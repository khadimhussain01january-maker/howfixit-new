A laptop that's hot enough to feel uncomfortable on your lap, or one that randomly shuts off during normal use, is almost always a cooling problem, not a "the laptop is dying" problem. The fix is usually cheaper and simpler than people expect.

## The first thing to check: airflow

Laptops pull air in from the bottom and push hot air out the side or back vents. If you've been using it on a bed, a couch cushion, or your actual lap, those vents are getting blocked, and the laptop has nowhere to send the heat it's generating. This alone causes a huge amount of what people assume is a hardware failure.

Put it on a hard, flat surface — a desk, a table, even a book — and see if the fan noise and heat calm down within a few minutes. If it does, that was most of your problem right there.

## Dust is the second most common cause

Open up the vents (usually along the back or sides) and look inside with a flashlight. If you see a gray, fuzzy buildup, that's dust clogging the heatsink fins, and it's blocking airflow the same way a blocked vent does, just permanently until it's cleaned.

- Turn the laptop off and unplug it
- Use a can of compressed air, and spray in short bursts, not one long blast
- Hold the fan blades still with a toothpick or similar while you spray, so they don't spin too fast and damage themselves
- Do this from a few inches away, aimed at the vent openings

If you're comfortable opening the bottom panel (a lot of laptops just need a few small screws removed), you can clean the fan and heatsink directly, which works a lot better than spraying through the vents from outside. If you're not comfortable opening it, the outside-vent method still helps.

## Check what's actually running

Open Task Manager (Ctrl+Shift+Esc on Windows) and sort by CPU. Sometimes overheating isn't really a cooling problem at all — it's one program pinning the CPU at 100% constantly, which generates far more heat than normal use. Browser tabs, background updates, and antivirus scans are common culprits. If you see something eating CPU that shouldn't be, closing it will often solve the "overheating" on its own.

## Cooling pads actually help

A basic USB cooling pad (the kind with a couple of fans built into a stand) genuinely makes a difference, especially for laptops that sit flat on a desk a lot. It's not a gimmick — it just gives the bottom vents somewhere for air to actually move instead of sitting against a solid desk surface.

## When it's the thermal paste

If the laptop is a few years old and none of the above helps much, the thermal paste between the CPU and the heatsink may have dried out — this is normal after 3-5 years and it's what actually transfers heat away from the chip. Repasting fixes this, but it does mean opening the laptop further than just the vents, so it's worth deciding whether that's a DIY job or one for a repair shop, depending on the laptop model.

## When to actually worry

If the laptop shuts off suddenly without warning, that's the machine protecting itself from real damage — treat that as a sign to deal with the cooling now, not eventually. Everyday warmth on the bottom, especially during heavier tasks like video or gaming, is normal and not something to worry about.
